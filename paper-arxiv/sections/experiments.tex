\subsection{Evaluation scope}

The experiments addressed three questions. First,
did the implementation agree with the analytic base target? Second, which
observations described that target and which depended on finite-time Langevin
dynamics? Third, what did the image, protein, financial, and masking examples
show within their measured scope? Novelty was one minus cosine similarity to
the nearest memory, so a larger value meant that an endpoint lay farther from
the stored bank. Diversity was the mean pairwise cosine distance, so a larger
value meant that the endpoint set was more dispersed. Energy was the model's
own Hopfield energy and was used only as an internal diagnostic. Noise can
increase novelty and diversity, and energies evaluated under different targets
are not a common quality scale. We therefore did not interpret any of these
three quantities as a stand-alone measure of realism or method superiority.
All-component exact sampling drew the categorical component from every stored
memory. We also used an initialization-matched diagnostic with five draws from
each of the 30 memories used to initialize the finite chains. That allocation
was not the stationary all-component law; it isolated the contribution of the
warm-start protocol. The code tests also compared the analytic score with
finite differences and recovered the nonuniform weights induced by unequal
memory norms.

\subsection{Exact target and implementation validation}

We first constructed a two-dimensional, three-component target with unequal
memory norms and inverse temperature $\beta=1.5$. The component weights, mean,
and covariance were available analytically. We compared 100,000 independent
exact draws with pooled samples from 16 unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA)
chains and 16 Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) chains. Each chain
ran for 100,000 steps; we discarded 20,000 steps and thinned by 20. Euclidean
error in the mean and Frobenius error in the covariance measured agreement with
the analytic moments, with smaller values indicating closer agreement. The
independent sampler recovered the moments to Monte Carlo precision
(Table~\ref{tab:exact-validation}). The adjusted chains remained close over the
tested step sizes. At $\alpha=0.01$, the MALA covariance error was larger
despite an acceptance rate that rounded to one; this result was consistent with highly
correlated small moves and showed why acceptance alone was not a convergence
diagnostic. The ULA covariance error increased at $\alpha=0.20$, revealing
finite-step bias. Because the chains were initialized from exact target draws,
this experiment tested the transition kernels and numerical implementation
rather than recovery from a poor initial state.

\subsection{Finite-step sensitivity on MNIST}

To test whether the finite-chain conclusions depended on the integration step,
we compared ULA and MALA on the same $K=100$ digit-3 memory at $\beta=2000$
at nine values of $\alpha$ between 0.001 and 0.5. The MALA acceptance rate
exceeded 95\% through $\alpha=0.02$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:stepsize-sweep}a), while the
absolute ULA--MALA differences in novelty, diversity, and energy remained small
in that range (Fig.~\ref{fig:stepsize-sweep}b). The differences increased
through $\alpha=0.05$ and $0.1$, while
the MALA acceptance rate fell to zero at $\alpha\geq0.2$. These results
supported the small-step protocol used in the main image experiment and showed
the expected finite-step sensitivity, but agreement between the two chains did not prove
that either chain mixed across all memory basins. The numerical values are
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-experiments}.

\subsection{Temperature and trajectory diagnostics}

The synthetic experiments analyzed responsibility
concentration and finite-time motion. For random unit memories with $d=64$ and
$K=16$, nearest-memory similarity and normalized responsibility entropy changed
smoothly over inverse temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:experiments}a). The observed
crossover was a finite-memory diagnostic, not a new storage-capacity boundary;
Appendix~\ref{app:responsibility-concentration} relates it to the asymptotic
condensation analysis of Lucibello and M\'ezard
\cite{lucibelloExponentialCapacityDense2024}. The finite ULA and longer-run ULA
energy distributions were similar in the $d=8$, $K=4$ comparison
(Fig.~\ref{fig:experiments}b), providing a finite-chain implementation check.
The analytic experiment in Table~\ref{tab:exact-validation}
provided the stronger equilibrium reference.
The temperature sweeps and single-chain experiments addressed a different
question: how did a finite trajectory move when initialized near a stored
pattern? At high $\beta$, the tested chains remained near their initial basin;
at lower $\beta$, their endpoints broadened and the nearest-memory labels
changed along the trajectory. These observations concerned finite-time kernels
and initialization, not additional expressiveness of the stationary base law.
On digit 8, decreasing $\beta$ first produced local textured variants and then
removed recognizable digit structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:temp-spectrum-digit8}).
The complete multi-digit, trajectory, and
load-ratio results are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:additional-experiments}
and~\ref{app:responsibility-concentration}.

\subsection{MNIST memory-neighborhood sampling}

We used the same memory bank of $K=100$ unit-normalized MNIST digit-3 images in
$d=784$. The finite ULA protocol launched 30 chains from distinct memories,
ran each chain for 5,000 steps at $\alpha=0.01$, discarded 2,000 steps, and
retained five thinned endpoints per chain. We compared those endpoints with
independent exact samples from all 100 mixture components. The exact uncertainty
is the standard deviation across 20 independent
sets of 150 endpoints. The ULA entries are the fixed point estimates from the
finite-chain experiment and do not have comparable repeated-run uncertainty.
The exact and ULA point estimates were close on these three diagnostics
(Table~\ref{tab:mnist-exact}). The
matched Parzen estimator was identical to the exact target by
Theorem~\ref{thm:exact-mixture}. The narrow Gaussian control in the earlier
baseline suite used the one-step innovation scale
$\sqrt{2\alpha/\beta}$ rather than the component scale $\beta^{-1/2}$, making
its standard deviation $7.07$ times too small at $\alpha=0.01$. Correcting the
scale removed the apparent distinction between that memory-perturbation model
and the base target. A local $k$-nearest-neighbor interpolation followed by
matched noise also produced nearby endpoint statistics. These results showed
that the base-target endpoints were local memory perturbations. Under matched
warm starts and random seeds, the paired ULA and MALA endpoints also looked
similar (Fig.~\ref{fig:mnist-baselines}b,c), but neither this visual agreement
nor agreement on the endpoint summaries proved equality of the finite ULA law
with the target distribution. For an exact draw from a unit memory, the approximation
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:novelty-approximation} predicted novelty 0.152 at $\beta=2000$
and 0.549 at $\beta=200$, close to the observed values. This calculation
explained why novelty increased as the component width increased; it did not
show that visibly noisier samples had higher perceptual quality. The full
baseline record, including bootstrap, fitted GMM-PCA, variational autoencoder
(VAE), diffusion, MALA, multi-digit, temperature, and data-scale experiments,
is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-experiments}. Those comparisons
document the tested low-data protocols but do not establish general superiority
over learned image generators.

\subsection{Protein sequence generation}

The protein experiment asked whether a memory-supported distribution could
produce candidate sequences that differed from 68 stored members of the
PF00076 RNA-recognition-motif family while preserving measured family
statistics. We one-hot encoded the aligned sequences, fitted a principal
component analysis (PCA) representation that retained 95\% of the variance,
normalized the resulting 59-dimensional memories, and decoded sampled vectors
with inverse PCA followed by a per-position argmax. The same 68 sequences
defined the memory bank, the PCA model, and the reference statistics, so this
was an in-sample distributional analysis rather than a held-out protein-design
test.
Six diagnostics characterized the endpoint sequences. Amino-acid KL (AA-KL)
measures divergence in global amino-acid composition, and positional KL
(Pos-KL) is the mean divergence of per-position amino-acid frequencies;
smaller values mean closer agreement with the stored family. Mutual-information
correlation (MI $r$) compares pairwise mutual-information matrices, with larger
values indicating closer linear agreement. The hidden Markov model (HMM) column
is the fraction passing the PF00076 profile threshold. Maximum sequence identity
(SeqID) is similarity to the closest stored sequence, whereas novelty is the
pre-decoding PCA-space distance from the nearest memory. The HMM statistic was
nondiscriminating because every tested method passed. Exact and finite-chain
sampling showed the same qualitative temperature trend and similar point
estimates for the base target, and the local-neighbor control was also close at
$\beta=8$ (Table~\ref{tab:protein-exact}). At that temperature, the perturbation
norm was large relative to a unit memory. Inverse PCA added the learned family
mean, and the argmax decoder
consequently favored consensus residues at conserved positions while retaining
variation at less-conserved positions. The decoded endpoints were closer to the
family consensus than the natural family members (Fig.~\ref{fig:protein}a),
while the nearest-memory identity distributions remained below exact replay
(Fig.~\ref{fig:protein}b). Thus part of the improved positional agreement
reflected regression toward the empirical mean rather than recovery of the full
natural variability. Within this limited scope, the memory-centered
distribution preserved the measured positional and pairwise statistics better
than the tested VAE at a similar novelty value. The result did not show that
Langevin dynamics was necessary, because exact sampling reproduced the
qualitative finding. It also did not test folding, RNA binding, biological
function, phylogenetically corrected coevolution, or held-out generalization.
The full preprocessing, decoder analysis, baseline table, and temperature
results are given in Appendices~\ref{app:protein-protocol} and
\ref{app:additional-experiments}.

\subsection{Conditional sampling by memory masking}

We tested the masking operation on 100 Olivetti faces from 10 subjects. The
unmasked finite-time procedure recovered the requested subject for 10.4\% of
endpoints, close to the 10\% uniform-subject rate, whereas setting every
off-subject multiplicity to zero increased recovery to 96.0\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:olivetti}b,c). Equation
\eqref{eq:exact-mixture} clarifies the mechanism: the hard mask removed
off-subject mixture components. The experiment therefore measured leakage from
a restricted memory subset; it did not show that the mask learned a new
conditional image model.

\subsection{Additional domain and finite-time analyses}

The supplementary material reports the multi-digit MNIST,
single-chain, protein, financial-return, step-size, Gaussian-control,
data-scaling, and multi-head experiments. These analyses remain useful for
understanding the tested finite-time kernels, preprocessing choices, and
failure modes. The financial study, for example, separated an independent
endpoint pool with marginal affine correction from a sequential MALA protocol;
the latter reproduced near-zero return autocorrelation but not volatility
clustering. The multi-head construction used independent tied-key/value
energies on specified PCA subspaces and a deterministic output map. Neither
experiment was used to claim that the base target was a general financial model
or that an arbitrary transformer head possessed the same scalar energy.
No experiment in this paper used a nontrivial smooth state-dependent
$C(\boldsymbol\xi)$. The constrained formulation in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:constrained-target} is therefore a theoretical extension and a
foundation for subsequent applications, not an empirical conclusion of the
present study.
