% introduction.tex -- Introduction

Attention is a central computational primitive of modern deep learning
\cite{vaswaniAttentionAllYou2017}. Given a query, it computes a
softmax-weighted average of stored values, a powerful but deterministic
operation. The same query and memory produce the same output, so attention
retrieves but does not by itself define a sampling procedure. Yet sampling from
a structured memory is useful in many downstream tasks: producing local
variants of stored prototypes, interpolating among related patterns, or
exploring states consistent with partial evidence. This need is especially
clear in low-data settings such as small protein families, rare-event
catalogues, and specialized image collections. These memory banks may contain
too few examples to train a reliable score network, but enough examples to
define a structured energy landscape. We therefore ask whether the attention
operation itself can supply the drift of a principled sampler over that
landscape.

Two established ideas suggest such a construction. Modern Hopfield networks
\cite{ramsauerHopfieldNetworksAll2021}, building on classical associative
memory \cite{hopfieldNeuralNetworksPhysical1982} and dense associative memories
\cite{krotovDenseAssociativeMemory2016,krotovLargeAssociativeMemory2021}, show
that tied-key/value attention is related to descent on a smooth, confining
energy. Langevin dynamics
\cite{robertsTweedieExponentialConvergence1996,wellingBayesianLearningStochastic2011,durmusNonasymptoticAnalysisUnadjusted2017}
turns a differentiable, normalizable energy into a sampler for its Boltzmann
distribution by adding calibrated noise to the gradient flow. Energy-based
models \cite{lecunTutorialEnergyBased2006,songHowTrainEnergyBased2021} and
score-based diffusion methods
\cite{songGenerativeModelingEstimating2019,songScoreBasedGenerativeModeling2021}
use related dynamics, but normally represent the score with a learned model.
The classical retrieval-generation duality, in which Hopfield networks retrieve
and Boltzmann machines sample
\cite{ackleyLearningAlgorithmBoltzmann1985}, motivates the corresponding
continuous construction for the modern Hopfield energy.

In this study, we develop what we call \emph{stochastic attention}. Applying
Langevin dynamics to the modern Hopfield energy produces an update with three parts: a
contraction of the current state, a softmax attention pull toward the stored
memories, and thermally calibrated Gaussian noise. The resulting trajectory
uses an analytic score and requires no learned score network or training loop.
Each step has the same asymptotic cost as tied single-query attention. The
construction does not apply to every transformer layer without modification:
the keys and values must occupy a common state space and define the scalar
energy used by the sampler.
The unmodified tied-key/value model also has a useful exact representation. Its
normalized Boltzmann distribution is an isotropic Gaussian mixture centered on
the memories. The attention weights are posterior component responsibilities,
and attention minus the current state gives the mixture score. Exact ancestral
sampling and continuous-time Langevin dynamics therefore describe the same
base distribution. Exact sampling is the efficient choice when the goal is an
independent endpoint from this base model, whereas a finite-step Langevin chain
exposes trajectory, initialization, and discretization effects. More
importantly, the mixture identity is a special case rather than the definition
of stochastic attention. Adding a differentiable observation, physical
penalty, likelihood, or design energy generally removes the direct ancestral
sampler, while its gradient can still be included in the stochastic-attention
drift.
We develop both levels of the construction. We give the complete
sampling pseudocode, regularity analysis, temperature diagnostics, masking
operation, finite-time trajectories, and a scoped multi-head extension. We
derive the exact Gaussian-mixture law for the base target and use independent
draws to calibrate the Langevin implementation. The responsibility-entropy
analysis gives a data-dependent description of attention concentration, rather
than a universal phase or quality threshold. Finally, we evaluate the method on
synthetic memories, handwritten digits, protein sequences, faces, and
financial returns. These experiments distinguish properties of the
memory-centered target from effects of numerical dynamics and decoding. In
particular, the protein study measures in-sample family statistics and does not
establish folding, function, or held-out generalization.
